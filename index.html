How BONE Works

What is BONE actually doing? +

In one sentence: BONE works out how likely each player is to win, turns that into fair odds, and compares those to the odds a bookmaker is offering. When the bookmaker's odds are more generous than they should be, that gap is your edge.

Think of it like knowing a coin lands heads 60% of the time, but someone's offering you a payout as if it only lands heads 50% of the time. Over many bets, taking that deal makes money. BONE is hunting for those mispriced "coins" in tennis matches.

What is ELO? +

ELO is a rating that measures how strong a player is, expressed as a single number. It was invented for chess and is now used everywhere from football to video games.

Every player starts at 1500. When you win, your rating goes up; when you lose, it goes down. The clever part: beating a strong player earns you more points than beating a weak one, and losing to a weak player costs you more than losing to a strong one. So the number naturally settles at a level that reflects how good you really are.

BONE builds these ratings by replaying every professional match from the last 3 years — about 14,000 matches — and updating each player's rating after every result. By the end, the numbers reflect current form. You can see the top 10 in the sidebar as a sanity check — the names at the top should be the players you'd expect.

How does it turn two ratings into a win probability? +

Once both players have a rating, the gap between them tells you who's favored and by how much. A small gap means a coin-flip; a big gap means a strong favorite.

The exact formula (you don't need to memorize it):

win chance = 1 / (1 + 10^((opponent − you) / 400))

The practical takeaway: a 400-point rating gap means the stronger player wins about 91% of the time. A 100-point gap is about 64%. Equal ratings = 50/50.

What's the "surface" adjustment? +

Tennis is played on three surfaces — hard, clay, and grass — and they play very differently. Some players are brilliant on clay but ordinary on grass (and vice versa). A single overall rating would miss that.

So BONE keeps four ratings per player: one overall, plus one for each surface. When it estimates a match, it blends them — roughly 60% overall rating and 40% the relevant surface rating.

This means a clay specialist gets a deserved bump when playing on clay, and gets marked down on grass where they're weaker. It's one of the main ways a tennis model can beat a naive one.

How is the "edge %" calculated? +

First, BONE converts its win probability into fair odds — what the odds should be if there were no bookmaker margin:

fair odds = 1 / win chance

So a 50% chance = fair odds of 2.00. A 67% chance = fair odds of 1.50.

Then you type in the actual odds the bookmaker is offering. The edge is how much value those odds give you versus your true chance:

edge % = (win chance × bookmaker odds − 1) × 100

If the result is positive, the bet is theoretically profitable long-term (+EV). If it's negative, the bookmaker is offering worse than fair and you should pass. BONE sorts every match by its biggest edge and color-codes them — bright amber for strong edges.

What do the stake modes mean? +

They're different rules for how much money to put on each bet.

Flat 1% — bet the same fixed amount (1% of your bankroll) every time, no matter the edge. Safest, simplest, almost impossible to blow up. Best place to start.

Kelly /2 and Kelly /4 — based on the Kelly Criterion, a formula that sizes bets according to your edge: bigger edge = bigger bet. Full Kelly is very aggressive and assumes your probabilities are perfect (they never are), so BONE uses fractions of it — a half (/2) or a quarter (/4) — to stay safe. The smaller the fraction, the smoother the ride.

Honest advice: stay on Flat 1% until you've proven the model actually works. Aggressive staking on an unproven model is how people go broke even when they technically have an edge.

Why are some "edges" huge — and why does BONE warn about them? +

A giant edge (say +60%) almost never means free money. It nearly always means the model is wrong about that match, not that the bookmaker is. The most common cause is thin data: if a player has only played a handful of recorded matches, their rating is unreliable, and the model can wildly misprice them.

BONE now handles this in two ways:

1. Rating shrinkage. A player rated on very few matches has their rating pulled toward the average (1500) until enough matches accumulate. A rating built on 3 matches isn't trusted like one built on 300. This stops unknown players from being treated as confident coin-flips.

2. Confidence labels + "why this edge?" Each match shows a confidence dot, and any match with a questionable prediction has a why this edge? link explaining the likely cause. Matches built on players the model barely knows get a LOW DATA or NO DATA verdict instead of a bet recommendation.

3. Fuzzy name matching. Player names differ across data sources — ESPN might write "Jesper Jong" while the ratings hold "Jesper de Jong". BONE now matches on surname (ignoring particles like "de"/"van") and first initial, so a known player isn't silently treated as unrated just because of a spelling difference. Genuine misses are clearly flagged.

The model was also tuned against ~4,000 held-out matches (see the backtest panel). Findings: a long-memory rating with K=24 and a 2-year recency half-life predicts best. Surface-specific ratings, margin-of-victory, and dynamic K were all tested and dropped — they added noise without improving accuracy. The honest takeaway: a real edge is usually small (2–6%). A massive one means the burden of proof is on the edge, not the bookmaker.

What are the set handicap odds? +

A set handicap bet adjusts the result by sets. The most common is −1.5 sets on the favourite (they must win 2–0 in a best-of-3) versus +1.5 sets on the underdog (they cover if they win even one set, or win the match).

BONE prices these from a relationship measured over thousands of past matches: how often a favourite of a given strength actually wins in straight sets. A slight favourite wins 2–0 less than 40% of the time; a heavy favourite does it around 70–88%. Multiplying that by the match-win probability gives the fair −1.5 price; the rest is the fair +1.5 price.

The "SET H'CAP" block under each match shows BONE's fair −1.5 and +1.5 prices, plus two input boxes. Tennis set-handicap odds aren't carried reliably by the odds APIs (they return moneyline only, or games handicaps), so you enter the book's set price yourself — read it off your bookmaker and type it in. BONE instantly computes the edge, applies the win-probability floor, and if it's a good bet it joins the ranked list tagged SETS (moneyline bets are tagged ML). Your entered prices are saved locally.

The win-probability floor applies to set bets too: a set side is only recommended if the model gives it at least your floor % chance to cash. So a +1.5 underdog bet whose cash-probability falls below the floor is skipped, just like a longshot moneyline.

Set bets you log are left pending for manual settlement — a +1.5 underdog can win the bet while losing the match, so they can't be auto-settled from the match result alone. And manual set prices aren't captured for closing-line (CLV) tracking, since that only works for auto-fetched odds.

Why no game handicaps (e.g. −4.5 games)? We tested it honestly. Pricing game lines needs a serve-level model, and backtesting showed that model only predicts serve performance ~11% better than guessing the tour average — too noisy to price half-game lines without inventing fake edges. Set handicaps are coarser and ride on match-win probability, which BONE predicts well, so those we trust.

What do the verdict labels mean? +

BONE turns each match's edge into a plain recommendation so you don't have to interpret the numbers yourself:

STRONG BET — edge of 7% or more. Clear value by the model's reckoning.

BET — edge of 3–7%. The realistic sweet spot where genuine +EV bets live.

LEAN — edge of 0.5–3%. Marginal, and within the model's own margin of error. Only worth it if you have extra conviction.

PASS — no value. The price is fair or against you. Most matches will be PASS, and that's correct.

CHECK THIS — edge above 15%. Counterintuitively, this is a warning. An edge that big usually means the odds are stale or something happened the model doesn't know about — an injury, a walkover, a withdrawal. Verify before betting.

LOW DATA / NO DATA — the edge comes from a player the model barely knows or doesn't know at all. Not a real betting opportunity; the number is unreliable.

The recommended stake next to each verdict uses whichever staking mode you've picked in the sidebar (Flat 1% or fractional Kelly), capped at 5% of bankroll for safety.

Where does the data come from? +

Historical matches: Jeff Sackmann's tennis database on GitHub — a free, public, widely-trusted record of professional matches going back decades. BONE loads the last 3 years of ATP and WTA results to build the ratings.

Today's matches: ESPN's public scoreboard, which lists scheduled and in-progress matches.

Odds: pulled automatically from The Odds API (the-odds-api.com). BONE shows the price from each bookmaker you've enabled — SportsBet and Ladbrokes by default — side by side, and highlights the best price for each player in amber. You can enable more books (TAB, Neds, Unibet, Betfair, Pinnacle) with one tap in the sidebar; up to 10 books costs just one API request. Everything is real, live data — nothing is made up or hardcoded.

⚠ Security reminder: right now the odds API key is stored in your browser and visible in the page. That's fine while BONE runs privately on your own machine. Before putting BONE on a public web address, the key must be moved to a hidden setup (a scheduled GitHub Action that fetches odds with the key kept secret), or anyone visiting could steal your key and drain your free quota. Ask about this when you build the next sport.

What are the model's limits? (read this) +

Being honest about what this can't do matters as much as what it can:

ELO is a strong baseline, not a crystal ball. It knows who's better and by how much, but it doesn't know about injuries, fatigue, travel, weather, head-to-head quirks, or a player having an off day. Professional bookmakers' lines already bake in a lot of this.

The model itself isn't your edge. Your edge comes from finding matches the bookmaker priced lazily — usually smaller events the big money ignores — and from shopping for the best odds across several books.

Surface detection is rough. BONE guesses the surface from the event name, which works for big tournaments but can mislabel smaller ones.

You won't know if it works until you measure. A handful of wins or losses is just luck. Tracking closing line value over hundreds of bets is the only honest test — that feature is coming.
